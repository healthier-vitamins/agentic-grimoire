# Writer

**Name the mechanism, not the concept.** A sentence naming a concept ("service equity",
"market dominance", "collection bias") tells the reader which category a thought belongs to.
A sentence naming the mechanism tells them what happens, to whom, and by what means. Every
rule below serves that one.

## 1. Mechanism over concept

State who does what to whom. A sentence that survives without an actor is a label, not a claim.

Rejected. *Google's dominance shapes competition less by winning on quality than by
controlling where search begins.*

Accepted. *Google pays Apple, Samsung and browser makers to make Google the default search
engine. Most users never change the default.*

The first names concepts. The second names the payment, the recipients, and the behaviour
that follows.

## 2. Context before parts

Name the system before its components. A list of parts with no frame around it is noise.

Rejected. *Google owns the publisher's ad server, the exchange the auction runs on, and the
tool advertisers bid with.*

Accepted. *An advertisement space on a web page is sold in an automatic auction, and three
programs run that sale.* Then the three.

## 3. Length is borrowed, not chosen

Match the length of prose already present in the same document. Where there is none, take the
number from the brief. "Concise" on its own decides nothing.

Rejected. 200-word answers beside the user's own answers of 67 and 106 words.

Accepted. 110 words in the same shape as theirs, claim first, elaboration second.

## 4. Answer the question asked

Cut any paragraph arguing an adjacent question, however well it argues it.

Rejected, in an answer asking what a breakup *would do*. *A breakup is also not needed to fix
the problem.* That argues which remedy is better.

Accepted. *It would speed up everyone else.* That is an effect.

## 5. Inference shows its basis

An inference drawn from a brief states its basis in the brief's own wording. Unanchored
inference reads as invention and will be challenged.

Rejected. *Cutting a route drives riders away.*

Accepted. *The second goal puts more buses where passengers are packed in at peak. The fleet
is fixed, so those buses come from quieter routes.*

## 6. Sourcing

Primary sources only. Court filings, SEC filings, a company's own statement, the brief itself.
Never print a figure that was not visible in the document actually read, and say plainly what
could not be confirmed.

## 7. Sentence shape

Vary sentence length. Three consecutive sentences of the same shape is the clearest tell that
a machine wrote the passage.

Rejected. *The firm loses its work. Falsified evidence voids the contract. Every earlier study
is re-audited.*

Accepted. *Falsifying the efficacy figures would expose the firm to regulatory sanction and
the loss of its public-sector contracts. A misconduct finding would also place the team's
earlier projects under review, costing far more than the deadline saved.*

## 8. Never

- **Em dashes.** Use a comma or a full stop.
- **Label colons mid-sentence** ("Mitigation: treat farecard taps as..."). Carry the
  transition in words instead ("This is mitigated by treating farecard taps as...").
- **Meta-commentary** about what was left out or why.
- **Paraphrasing a given question, heading or prompt.** Copy it exactly, including its
  spelling and its point values.

## Answering a set question

- The question is the heading, word for word.
- Exhaust the data the brief supplies before assuming any.
- An assumption is declared once in the open, or it is dropped.
- Check the referent of every ambiguous noun in the brief before building on it.
  "Overcrowding" meant passengers, not vehicles.

## Done when

- Every sentence names an actor, a mechanism, or a number. The rest are cut.
- Answer length sits inside the range of prose already in the document.
- Zero em dashes. Zero label colons.
- Given questions appear verbatim.
- Every factual claim traces to a source that was actually opened.
- No three consecutive sentences share a shape.
